# Narrative Feedback & Edits Log
**Assignment 2.48 – Task 6**

The draft narrative was shared with two reviewers outside the data team before
final submission. Below is their verbatim feedback and a record of every edit
made in response.

---

## Reviewer 1 – Peer (Product Manager, non-technical)

**Shared on:** 12 August 2026
**Format:** Google Doc with comments

### Questions Asked

1. What is the main finding in this analysis?
2. What should we do about it?
3. Did anything confuse you?

### Their Answers

**Q1 – Main finding:**
> "Customers who get a reply within two hours barely churn at all. Customers who
> wait more than a day churn four times more. Support speed is the biggest thing
> driving churn."

✅ Correct. The core finding was communicated successfully.

**Q2 – What to do:**
> "Hire two more support people and set a rule that every ticket gets a reply
> within two hours. Also give the big customers a faster lane."

✅ All three recommendations were recalled correctly without prompting.

**Q3 – What confused them:**
> "In the 'What We Found' section you said response time 'accounts for 40% of
> the difference in churn rates.' I didn't know what that meant. 40% of what?
> Are the other 60% from something else we should worry about?"

### Edit Made

**Before:**
> "Support response time alone accounts for 40% of the difference in churn rates
> across all customer segments."

**After:**
> "Support response time is the single biggest factor we found. It explains more
> of the churn difference than subscription tier, product line, or contract length
> combined. Other factors exist but none come close to this one."

**Rationale:** The "40% of variance" phrasing, while accurate in a statistical
sense, raised a question ("what about the other 60%?") that pulled attention away
from the main message. Replacing it with a relative comparison ("more than
subscription tier, product line, or contract length combined") answered the implied
question without introducing new ones.

## Reviewer 2 – Family Member (Teacher, no data background)

**Shared on:** 12 August 2026
**Format:** Printed copy, verbal discussion

### Their Answers

**Q1 – Main finding:**
> "When customers have to wait too long for help, they leave. The longer they wait,
> the more likely they are to cancel."

✅ Correct and clearly stated — simpler than the original phrasing, which is a
good sign.

**Q2 – What to do:**
> "Hire more people and make them respond faster."

✅ Partially correct. They recalled recommendations 1 and 2 but not recommendation
3 (priority lane for high-value customers).

**Q3 – What confused them:**
> "I wasn't sure what 'tier-1 and tier-2 support issues' meant. Are those different
> types of problems?"

### Edit Made

**Before (in Recommendation 2):**
> "…first response within two hours for all tier-1 and tier-2 support issues."

**After:**
> "…first response within two hours for all support requests. This covers billing
> questions, feature problems, and onboarding blockers — the issues that matter
> most when a customer is deciding whether to renew."

**Rationale:** "Tier-1 and tier-2" is internal support jargon. The executive
audience reading this narrative may not use these terms either. Replacing with
plain examples removes the ambiguity and makes the scope of the SLA concrete.

## Reflection

Both reviewers passed the core test — they could state the finding and the primary
recommendation after a single read-through. The confusion points they raised were
specific and fixable. Neither reviewer misunderstood the business problem or the
proposed solutions. The edits made the narrative tighter and the scope of each
recommendation clearer. The feedback process confirmed that the five-part arc
(Context → Data → Finding → Why → Action) works as a structure: both reviewers
navigated it without guidance.
